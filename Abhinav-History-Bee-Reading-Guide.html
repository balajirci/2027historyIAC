1 · Greece: rival cities, common enemy

Greek speakers shared language, religious festivals and stories, but lived in separate poleis, or city-states. Mountains and the sea helped shape that pattern. Before classical Greece, the Minoans flourished on Crete, where Knossos was a major centre. The Mycenaeans built mainland palace centres, wrote Greek in Linear B, and form part of the Bronze Age background to the Trojan War tradition.

Athens developed direct democracy: eligible citizens could vote in the assembly themselves. Citizenship excluded women, enslaved people and resident foreigners. Sparta had two kings and other governing bodies, including ephors. Its citizen army depended on the labour of the helots. Think assembly and fleet for Athens; helots and military training for Sparta.

BattleClue chain
Marathon, 490 BCEFirst Persian invasion → Darius I → Miltiades and the Athenian victory.
Thermopylae, 480 BCEXerxes → narrow land pass → Leonidas and the Spartan-led defence. Persia won.
Salamis, 480 BCENarrow strait → Themistocles → Greek fleet defeats Persian fleet.
Plataea, 479 BCEGreek land victory → Persian commander Mardonius killed.

After the Persian Wars, Athens led the Delian League and increasingly controlled its allies. Under Pericles, Athens rebuilt the Acropolis, including the Parthenon. Competition with Sparta and its allies erupted in the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BCE). Athens lost after setbacks including the Sicilian Expedition and the naval defeat at Aegospotami.

Recall: Which battle pairs Leonidas with a land pass, and which pairs Themistocles with a strait?Thermopylae and Salamis, respectively.
Recall: Which war pitted Athens against Sparta?The Peloponnesian War. Both cities had fought on the Greek side against Persia earlier.

2 · Alexander: conquest and its aftermath

Philip II of Macedon built the military and political base that his son inherited. His victory at Chaeronea (338 BCE) established Macedonian dominance over much of Greece. Alexander III, tutored by Aristotle, succeeded him in 336 BCE. Alexander invaded the Persian Empire, beat Darius III at Issus (333 BCE), and defeated him decisively at Gaugamela (331 BCE). He founded Alexandria in Egypt, reached the Indian subcontinent and fought King Porus at the Hydaspes (326 BCE). He died at Babylon in 323 BCE.

His generals, the Diadochi, fought for control. The Ptolemies ruled Egypt and the Seleucids controlled much of western Asia. The Hellenistic world that followed brought Greek language and institutions into contact with local traditions. Alexandria became a centre of scholarship as well as a capital.

Clue split: Chaeronea and the rise of Macedon → Philip II. Issus, Gaugamela and Porus → Alexander. Successor kingdoms and Alexandria’s scholarship → Hellenistic era.

Recall: Which battle connects Porus to Alexander?Hydaspes.
Recall: Who ruled Egypt after the empire broke up?The Ptolemies, beginning with Ptolemy I.

3 · Myth and culture: learn the relationships

Mythology questions often name a helper, a weapon or a place before naming the central character. Study each story as a chain. These are myth traditions with variants; keep them separate from dated historical events.

Daedalus and IcarusFather and son escape with wings; Icarus flies too close to the sun.

For Greek culture, connect Socrates to questioning and his trial, Plato to the Academy and Republic, and Aristotle to the Lyceum and Alexander. Connect the tragedies Oresteia, Antigone and Medea to Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides, respectively. Aristophanes is a comedian; look for Lysistrata or The Clouds.

Recall: Who gives the thread, and who builds the Labyrinth?Ariadne gives the thread; Daedalus builds the Labyrinth.
Recall: Which playwright wrote Medea? Which philosopher taught Alexander?Euripides and Aristotle.

4 · Rome: the Republic and the Punic Wars

Rome grew on the Tiber, with access to farmland and Mediterranean routes. The traditional founding story names Romulus and Remus; the traditional start date for the Republic is 509 BCE. Under the Republic, two consuls served as leading annual magistrates. The Senate had great influence, and assemblies elected officials. Plebeians won protections including tribunes and written laws known as the Twelve Tables.

WarWhat changed
First, 264–241 BCERome and Carthage fought over Sicily. Rome developed its naval capacity and gained Sicily after victory.
Second, 218–201 BCEHannibal crossed the Alps and won at Cannae (216 BCE). Scipio defeated him at Zama (202 BCE) in North Africa.
Third, 149–146 BCERome besieged and destroyed Carthage in 146 BCE. Scipio Aemilianus commanded the final siege.

Conquest brought wealth, enslaved labour and powerful generals. Land reformers Tiberius and Gaius Gracchus faced violent opposition. The Republic then experienced civil wars and extraordinary commands. The alliance of Caesar, Pompey and Crassus is called the First Triumvirate. Caesar crossed the Rubicon in 49 BCE, defeated Pompey at Pharsalus and became dictator. He was assassinated in 44 BCE on the Ides of March.

Clue split: Cannae → Hannibal wins. Zama → Scipio Africanus wins. Destruction of Carthage → Scipio Aemilianus, a later commander.

Recall: Why can Hannibal be associated with both a great victory and a decisive defeat?He defeated Rome at Cannae, but Scipio defeated him at Zama.
Recall: Who crossed the Rubicon, and against which rival did he fight at Pharsalus?Julius Caesar crossed; Pompey was his rival.

5 · Rome: emperors and the clues that identify them

Caesar’s assassination did not restore lasting republican government. His adopted heir Octavian joined Mark Antony and Lepidus in the Second Triumvirate, then defeated Antony and Cleopatra’s forces at Actium (31 BCE). In 27 BCE the Senate gave him the title Augustus. Roman rule abroad had expanded long before this, but Augustus marks the conventional start of rule by emperors.

EmperorClues
AugustusOctavian, Actium, first emperor, start of the Pax Romana.
ClaudiusRoman invasion of Britain in 43 CE.
NeroGreat Fire of Rome in 64 CE; persecution of Christians afterwards. The fiddle story is a legend.
Vespasian / TitusVespasian began the Colosseum; Titus inaugurated it. Vesuvius erupted in 79 CE during Titus’s reign.
Trajan / HadrianTrajan: Dacia, column, expansion. Hadrian: wall in Britain, consolidation.
Marcus AureliusStoic emperor, Meditations, succeeded by Commodus.
Diocletian / ConstantineDiocletian: Tetrarchy and persecution. Constantine: Milvian Bridge, Nicaea and Constantinople.

Roads served armies, trade and government. Aqueducts supplied cities with water; arches, concrete and vaults helped Romans build large public structures. Roman citizenship and law connected provinces to the state, even as people kept many local traditions.

Recall: Caesar or Augustus—which one is conventionally called the first emperor?Augustus. Julius Caesar was dictator and was assassinated before Augustus’s settlement.
Recall: Which emperor links Dacia and a column? Which one links Britain and a wall?Trajan and Hadrian, respectively.

6 · Christianity, the fall of the West and Byzantium

Christianity arose in a Jewish setting in Roman-era Judea. The movement centred on Jesus of Nazareth and spread through communities across the empire. Paul helped spread it among non-Jews; his letters became part of the New Testament. Imperial persecution varied over time and place. Diocletian’s reign saw a major persecution beginning in 303 CE.

Constantine won at the Milvian Bridge in 312. In 313, Constantine and Licinius agreed to religious toleration, conventionally called the Edict of Milan. Constantine convened the Council of Nicaea in 325 and dedicated Constantinople in 330. The Edict of Thessalonica in 380 under Theodosius I established Nicene Christianity as imperial orthodoxy. Keep the toleration and official-orthodoxy clues separate.

The Western Empire lost territory and revenue and faced military and political instability. Alaric’s Visigoths sacked Rome in 410; Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus in 476, the conventional end date for the Western Empire. The Eastern Roman Empire continued. Under Justinian, Roman law was compiled, Belisarius led reconquests, and the Hagia Sophia was rebuilt. In 1453, Ottoman sultan Mehmed II captured Constantinople.

Timeline: 313 toleration → 325 Nicaea → 330 Constantinople dedicated → 380 Nicene orthodoxy → 476 Western imperial endpoint → 1453 Constantinople falls.

Recall: Which emperor goes with Nicaea, and which one goes with the Edict of Thessalonica?Constantine and Theodosius I, respectively.
Recall: Why do both 476 and 1453 appear in answers about Rome’s fall?476 marks the conventional end of Western imperial rule. The Eastern Roman Empire survived until Constantinople fell in 1453.
