African History

  • Egypt became the first Arab state to recognize Israel under the leadership of President Anwar Sadat (1918-1981).
  • In 2003, Sudanese dictator Omar al-Bashir (1944 - present) launched a Darfur counter-insurgency campaign, killing 300,000 civilians.
  • The government of South Africa has been led by the African National Congress since the end of apartheid in 1994.

Ancient History

  • The Crisis of the Third Century, a destructive 50-year period, was ended by Diocletian (242/243-311/312).
  • Mycenae is an archaeological site in Greece that was home to the legendary king Agamemnon.
  • Theodosius the Great (347-395) was a Roman emperor who made Nicene Christianity the official state religion of the Roman Empire.

Asian History

  • Bangladesh was led by Sheikh Hasina (1947 - present) from 1996 to 2001 and again for four consecutive terms from 2009 until her resignation in August 2024.
  • The eruption of Krakatoa in 1883 created the loudest sound recorded in history.
  • The collapse of the Han dynasty was followed by an era of civil war called the Three Kingdoms period.
  • The Mongol Empire was established by Genghis Khan.
  • The Indus Valley Civilization spanned modern-day Pakistan, northwestern India, and northeastern Afghanistan.
  • Sukarno (1901-1970) led the nationalist movement of Indonesia before becoming the country’s first president in 1945.
  • The beginning of the Korean War was marked by an invasion by North Korean forces as they crossed the 38th parallel.
  • A noblewoman named Murasaki Shikibu wrote what is widely considered the world’s first novel, the Tale of Genji.

European History

  • The Velvet Divorce of 1993 resulted in the dissolution of Czechoslovakia into the Czech Republic and Slovakia.
  • The RAF successfully defended the United Kingdom against the Luftwaffe during the Battle of Britain.
  • Maria Theresa (1717-1780) was the only female ruler to govern the Habsburg Domain.
  • The Hanseatic League consisted of a network of merchant guilds in Northern and Central Europe.
  • England’s Catholic king James the Second was replaced with Protestant Mary the Second and William the Third in the Glorious Revolution.
  • The Spanish Armada was sent by King Philip the Second of Spain in 1588 to overthrow Queen Elizabeth the First of England.
  • The British East India Company was established in 1600 and held a monopoly on trade with Asia until it was dissolved in 1874.
  • The Reign of Terror during the French Revolution was primarily led by radical Jacobin leader Maximilien Robespierre.
  • British explorer James Cook (1728-1779) was the first European to visit the Hawaiian Islands and mapped New Zealand and the eastern coastline of Australia.
  • The Provisional Government of Russia was overthrown by the Bolsheviks in the October Revolution.
  • The Iron Curtain was a physical, political, and ideological barrier that split Europe into two separate halves during the Cold War.
  • The Warsaw Pact was a military alliance between the Soviet Union and seven Eastern Bloc socialist republics during the Cold War.
  • The Crimean War was a conflict from 1853 to 1856 where Russia fought against an alliance of the Ottoman Empire, France, Great Britain.

Latin American and Caribbean History

  • The Sandinistas, a guerilla group and political party in Nicaragua, overthrew the Somoza family dictatorship in 1979.
  • Juan Perón’s (1895-1974) namesake political movement, Peronism, focused on three pillars: social justice, economic independence, and political sovereignty. It is still popular today in Argentina.
  • Disputes over the ownership of the Falkland Islands resulted in a 1982 war between the United Kingdom and Argentina. The vast majority of its citizens are descended from British settlers.
  • Beginning in 1667, the Dutch colonized Suriname and ruled it for nearly 300 years until the country achieved full independence in 1975.
  • Toussaint Louverture (1743-1803) led the enslaved people of Haiti in a successful revolt against French colonial rule and slavery.

Canadian and U.S. History

  • Lyndon B. Johnson (1908-1973) signed the landmark Civil Rights Act of 1964, which prohibited discrimination and segregation in public places based on color, race, and religion.
  • The Haida people have lived on Haida Gwaii, an island group off the coast of British Columbia, for thousands of years.
  • Prince Edward Island officially joined Canada as the seventh province on July 1, 1873.
  • Theodore Roosevelt (1858-1919) formed the Bull Moose Party in 1912 after losing the Republican Party presidential nomination to William Howard Taft (1857-1930).
  • The Klondike Gold Rush resulted in a massive migration of about 100,000 people journeying to the Yukon region between 1896 and 1899 after gold was discovered there.
  • Soldiers of the Continental Army endured a harsh winter while encamped at Valley Forge during the American Revolution.
  • Jacques Cartier (1491-1557) was a Breton-born explorer who gave the name “Canada” to the land area around the Saint Lawrence River.
  • Hudson Bay was named after Henry Hudson (c. 1565-1611), who sailed there in 1610 while trying to find a sea route to Asia.
  • The Sixties Scoop was a period in Canada from the late 1950s to the 1980s when child welfare authorities took thousands of Indigenous children from their families and communities.
  • Emily Carr (1871-1945) was a famous Canadian painter and writer who captured the wild beauty of the Pacific Northwest forests and Indigenous totem poles.
  • Newfoundland officially joined Canada on March 31, 1949, as the 10th province until it was officially renamed to Newfoundland and Labrador in 2001.
  • The end of the 1935 On-to-Ottawa Trek was marked by the Regina Riot, a violent clash between police and protesters.
  • Sir John A. Macdonald (1815-1891) was the first prime minister of Canada and the main leader who united separate colonies into a single nation in 1867.

The Canadian Core, as a story

Founding and the province sequence

Canada was made in pieces, and the joining order is itself a testable list. Confederation came on July 1, 1867, uniting Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia and New Brunswick under the British North America Act, negotiated at Charlottetown and Quebec City in 1864. Manitoba was created in 1870 out of the Red River crisis. British Columbia joined in 1871 on the promise of a transcontinental railway. Prince Edward Island, which had hosted the first conference, held out until railway debt forced it in on July 1, 1873, as the seventh province. Alberta and Saskatchewan were carved from the North-West Territories on one day, September 1, 1905. Newfoundland came last, on March 31, 1949, after two referendums, and was renamed Newfoundland and Labrador in 2001.

The men who made the 1867 deal: Sir John A. Macdonald, the dominant strategist and first prime minister, and George-Etienne Cartier, his Quebec partner, who convinced French Canada that federation would protect its language, law and faith. Remember the trap built into the surname: Jacques Cartier is the explorer of the 1530s who named Canada; George-Etienne Cartier is the politician of the 1860s.

The West: fur, rebellion, railway

The Hudson's Bay Company, chartered in 1670, ran the fur trade across Rupert's Land for two centuries. When it surrendered that land to Canada in 1869 without consulting the Metis of Red River, Louis Riel's provisional government seized Fort Garry and negotiated Manitoba into existence - the Red River Rebellion. The Pacific Scandal of 1873, in which Macdonald's party took railway money from Sir Hugh Allan, briefly destroyed his government. Fifteen years after Red River, grievances over land and the vanished buffalo produced the North-West Rebellion of 1885 in Saskatchewan, ended at Batoche; the nearly finished Canadian Pacific Railway rushed troops west and proved its worth, and its last spike was driven at Craigellachie on November 7, 1885. Riel was hanged at Regina that November and is now honored as a founder of Manitoba.

The War of 1812 trio

Canada's founding war story has three names. Major-General Isaac Brock, the "Saviour of Upper Canada", bluffed Detroit into surrender in August 1812 and died at Queenston Heights that October. His ally Tecumseh, the Shawnee leader building an Indigenous confederacy, was killed at the Battle of the Thames in 1813, and the confederacy died with him. Laura Secord walked some thirty kilometres through the woods in June 1813 to warn the British at Beaver Dams of an American attack she had overheard in her own house. Brock wins battles, Tecumseh holds the alliance, Secord carries the warning - keep the three roles separate.

Prime ministers worth a card each

  • Wilfrid Laurier (1896-1911): first francophone prime minister; "sunny ways" compromise on the Manitoba Schools Question; the great immigration boom.
  • Robert Borden (1911-1920): the First World War prime minister; conscription in 1917 and the khaki election; Canada signs Versailles separately.
  • R. B. Bennett (1930-1935): the Depression prime minister; the "Bennett buggy"; relief camps; created the Bank of Canada; the man the On-to-Ottawa Trek marched against in 1935.
  • Mackenzie King (1921-1948, in three runs): the longest-serving prime minister; the King-Byng Affair; "not necessarily conscription, but conscription if necessary".
  • John Diefenbaker (1957-1963): the 1958 landslide; the Canadian Bill of Rights, 1960; cancelled the Avro Arrow on "Black Friday", February 20, 1959.
  • Lester Pearson (1963-1968): Nobel Peace Prize for peacekeeping at Suez; the Maple Leaf Flag in 1965; the Canada Pension Plan.
  • Pierre Elliott Trudeau (1968-1979, 1980-1984): the Official Languages Act; the October Crisis and "just watch me"; patriation and the Charter in 1982.
  • Brian Mulroney (1984-1993): free trade with the US, then NAFTA; the failed Meech Lake (1990) and Charlottetown (1992) accords; the GST.
  • Jean Chretien (1993-2003): the Red Book platform; the deficit eliminated; the 1995 referendum and then the Clarity Act; kept Canada out of Iraq in 2003.
  • Stephen Harper (2006-2015): first leader of the merged Conservative Party; the GST cut; the 2008 apology for residential schools.
  • Justin Trudeau (2015-2025): won from third place in 2015; cannabis legalization and carbon pricing; resigned in 2025, succeeded by Mark Carney.

Quebec: from the Quiet Revolution to the Clarity Act

The chain runs in one direction and each link is a card. The Quiet Revolution of the 1960s modernized and secularized Quebec under Jean Lesage - Hydro-Quebec nationalized under "Maitres chez nous", schools and hospitals taken from the Church - and created the confidence that produced the sovereignty movement. Rene Levesque founded the Parti Quebecois in 1968, won power in 1976 and passed Bill 101 in 1977. The FLQ's October Crisis of 1970, the kidnappings of James Cross and Pierre Laporte and the War Measures Act, was the violent fringe of the same era, not its mainstream. The 1980 referendum on sovereignty-association lost 60-40. The Meech Lake Accord died in 1990. The 1995 referendum, under Jacques Parizeau with Lucien Bouchard as its star campaigner, lost by 50.58 to 49.42 percent; Parizeau's concession blamed "money and the ethnic vote". Ottawa's answer was the Supreme Court's Secession Reference in 1998 and the Clarity Act in 2000: no unilateral secession, but a clear majority on a clear question must be negotiated.

Rights, schools and apologies

Two systems removed Indigenous children, and they must not be merged. Residential schools were church-run, government-funded boarding institutions, from the 1880s to the last closure in 1996, attended by some 150,000 children; the Truth and Reconciliation Commission called the system cultural genocide, and Stephen Harper apologized in Parliament in 2008. The Sixties Scoop was child welfare: from the late 1950s to the 1980s, authorities took Indigenous children into foster and adoptive homes, many outside Canada. Alongside them stand the rights milestones: Manitoba women first won the vote in 1916 under Nellie McClung's campaign; the Famous Five won the Persons Case in 1929, establishing that women are "persons" under the constitution; Viola Desmond's 1946 stand in a New Glasgow theatre became a civil rights landmark, and she is now on the ten-dollar bill.

War, invention and icons

The Canadian war canon for this level: the Battle of the Plains of Abraham in 1759, where Wolfe and Montcalm both died and New France began to fall; Vimy Ridge in April 1917, where the four Canadian divisions attacked together; John McCrae writing "In Flanders Fields" at Ypres in 1915; the Halifax Explosion of December 6, 1917; the Dieppe Raid of August 1942, a one-day disaster whose lessons fed D-Day; Juno Beach on June 6, 1944, the Canadian D-Day sector; and Ortona at Christmas 1943, where Canadians invented "mouse-holing" through house walls. Science and symbols: Banting and Best's insulin at Toronto in 1921-22, sold for a one-dollar patent; Bell conceiving the telephone at Brantford; the Avro Arrow, cancelled 1959; L'Anse aux Meadows, the Norse site in Newfoundland proving Europeans reached America around the year 1000; Champlain founding Quebec City in 1608; Emily Carr and the Group of Seven defining Canadian landscape art; the Gouzenko Affair of 1945 opening the Cold War in Ottawa; Expo 67 as the centennial's coming-out party; Terry Fox's Marathon of Hope in 1980; and Wayne Gretzky, whose 1988 trade to Los Angeles was treated as a national event. Tommy Douglas, Saskatchewan's premier, built medicare in one province and was voted the Greatest Canadian - the model Pearson's Canada then adopted.
